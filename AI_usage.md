## Task 1 — Variational Autoencoder

### Implementation

The initial implementation of the encoder/decoder architecture, the reparameterisation
trick, the BCE + KL divergence loss, the training loop, and the three visualisations
(loss curve, reconstruction comparison, latent manifold sweep) was generated by Claude
as part of the conversation.

Before delivering the code, Claude ran a forward and backward pass of the architecture
against the actual OASIS slices I had uploaded, to confirm correct tensor shapes and that
gradients computed successfully. I verified the results independently by running the full
training on Rangpur.

### What I am responsible for and can explain

- Why the KL divergence term is what makes the latent space continuous, and therefore what
  makes interpolating between latent points produce smoothly varying brain slices rather
  than disconnected outputs.
- Why the reparameterisation trick is necessary — sampling is not differentiable, so the
  randomness is moved into a separate `eps` term to allow gradients to flow back through
  the encoder.
- Why a separate 2-dimensional latent model is trained purely for the manifold
  visualisation (a 16-dimensional latent space cannot be swept and rendered directly as a
  2D image grid).
- Why VAE reconstructions are characteristically blurry — the tradeoff between the
  reconstruction and KL terms in the loss.

### Interpreting the results

I ran the training to completion and shared the real output (loss values across 40 epochs
and the three generated figures) back with Claude for a second opinion on whether the
results were correct. Points raised in that discussion that I have carried into my own
understanding:

- Train loss (1036) and validation loss (1053) tracking closely together indicates the
  model is generalising rather than memorising the training slices.
- The loss plateau from around epoch 30 indicates training ran long enough, rather than
  stalling prematurely.
- **The degraded, noisy samples in the upper-right of the manifold grid** are not a bug.
  That region is far from the origin of the latent space, where the KL prior concentrates
  the training encodings — so the decoder is extrapolating into a region it saw little
  data from, rather than interpolating between known points.

## Task 2 — UNet Segmentation

### Implementation

The UNet architecture (encoder/bottleneck/decoder with skip connections), the combined
cross-entropy + soft Dice loss, the per-class DSC metric, the training loop with
validation-based checkpointing, and the visualisation code were generated by Claude.

Before delivering the code, Claude inspected the actual segmentation masks from the
dataset I uploaded to determine the label encoding (raw PNG values 0 / 85 / 170 / 255),
then ran the full pipeline end-to-end on a small local subset to confirm the data
loading, training loop, DSC computation, figure generation and checkpoint saving all
worked. It also ran a deliberate overfitting test on a handful of images to confirm the
per-class DSC scores actually climbed, verifying the architecture, loss and metric were
correctly wired before I committed GPU time to a full run.

I ran the full training on Rangpur myself (20 epochs, 922 seconds on an A100) and
verified the results.

### What I am responsible for and can explain

- **Skip connections**: each decoder stage concatenates the matching encoder feature map
  onto its upsampled input. Downsampling discards fine spatial detail; the skip
  connections reintroduce it, which is what allows precise boundary delineation rather
  than blurry approximate regions.
- **Categorical (one-hot) output**: the final 1×1 convolution produces one channel per
  class; softmax over the channel dimension gives a per-pixel probability distribution,
  and `argmax` gives the predicted class. This satisfies the lab sheet's requirement for
  categorical output.
- **Why the loss combines cross-entropy and Dice**: cross-entropy handles per-pixel
  classification but is dominated by the large background class; the Dice term directly
  optimises the metric being reported and is far less sensitive to class imbalance, which
  matters for small classes like CSF.
- **Why masks are resized with nearest-neighbour interpolation**: bilinear interpolation
  would produce intermediate values between label values (e.g. 127 between 85 and 170),
  which correspond to no real class and would corrupt the ground truth.
- **Why CSF scores lowest** (0.9578, versus 0.9790 for white matter): CSF forms the
  thinnest and smallest structures in the image, so boundary pixels are a larger fraction
  of the class total and small misalignments cost proportionally more.
- **Why validation is used for checkpoint selection and the test set is evaluated only
  once**: selecting a model using the test set would make the reported score an optimistic,
  biased estimate of performance on genuinely unseen data. This distinction was clarified
  by the teaching team on the discussion board and applied here and in Part 3.2.
